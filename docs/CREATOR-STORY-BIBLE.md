# FREAK // CITY — Corey’s creator story bible

**Full spoilers · current authored project reviewed 17 September 2026**

Read the story below first. Use the companion [scene-by-scene creator’s guide](CREATOR-SCENE-GUIDE.md) when you want to change a scene, strengthen a character or place a joke. The guide accounts for all **117 registered authored scenes**, plus the newer envelope arrangement and smaller interactions. You do not need to read every dialogue branch to understand the game.

This describes the local working tree on `feature/state-driven-visuals`, including uncommitted work after `0128fe4`. The text-led game is the active direction. The graphical prototype remains an inactive experiment; visual development and publication remain on hold.

Status labels throughout:

- **Implemented:** material exists in current executable content or behavior. “Authored” means a scene and its choices/effects are registered; it does **not** certify that every natural sentence or route through the current text interface works. Where that difference matters, it is stated.
- **Documented plan:** a design intention or character-background statement in project documents which the runtime does not itself establish. This includes established design-only background, not just speculative future features.
- **Unresolved:** the sources leave a question open, contradict one another or do not provide a dependable playable connection.
- **New suggestion:** an editorial proposal in this document. It is not canon or an implementation instruction.

## 1. THE STORY FROM BEGINNING TO END

### An invitation has already made a decision about you

**Implemented — authored first-night story.** You arrive outside Velvet late on a wet Friday night. An unsigned message says to come alone and not give your real name. You have never been to this club, yet its address is already saved in your maps under a name you do not recognise. The invitation bears 23:41. The city appears to know something about your involvement that you have not been told.

Velvet is both a nightlife venue and a workplace. Its people have repairs to finish, food going cold and buses to catch. Inez, the custodian, asks for the name you want used inside. Mara, the bar manager, offers a drink of dubious quality. Celeste owns the building and can offer access upstairs. Luca runs a radio archive and is having a less successful relationship with a wall socket. The oddness of the invitation sits alongside ordinary embarrassment, attraction and tired people making each other laugh.

You can tell Inez you do not know the sender, give only an alias, or claim Luca invited you. That last claim becomes something she can pass on: a lie told to get through a door can return as an uncomfortable conversation with the person whose name you borrowed. None of these approaches changes who really sent the invitation.

### The ledger puts other people into the same predicament

The club is involved with a tenant relief fund, the Umbrella. Its original ledger records names, addresses, payments and obligations. Some recipients used names they no longer use. Some entries turn loans into grants, or grants into advances; some obligations outlive the people who first agreed to them. The record can expose institutional wrongdoing. It can also expose people who needed help and did not agree to have their lives made public.

Mara wants the names protected. Luca wants evidence strong enough to make the archive impossible to dismiss. Celeste wants the transfer handled within terms she can influence. Inez knows how a practical record can become an authority it never deserved.

Your own involvement is an example. You once signed for a delivery in your apartment building. That signature has been reused as permission to act as an independent witness to future transfers. You did not knowingly consent. The club can record an objection; that does not necessarily stop the transaction. Being able to complain is not the same as having had a choice.

The apparently impossible timestamp has a mundane answer. The old printer produced the invitation before Inez disconnected it. **23:41 is the delivery header, not the printing time.** Machinery and misleading paperwork explain this clue; the story does not establish a supernatural event.

### Two invitations divide the night

At 00:20, an upstairs exchange takes place. At 00:26, Ruth, a worker who saw the archive moved, leaves on the last bus. The authored structure makes these competing opportunities: attend the official event, hear the witness outside, or decline both.

Upstairs, the conflict is over how your presence will be described. You can challenge the reused signature, observe custody, put an objection in writing, bargain for a copy or leave. Celeste reveals that the board already has a duplicate. Her willingness to object to the board does not absolve her of her own use of institutional power.

Outside, Ruth’s delivery docket places the archive box’s movement at 00:19, before the supposedly first custody transfer at 00:20. That establishes an inconsistency. It does not establish who ordered the movement or prove every ledger entry false. Ruth wants to catch her bus, not become the permanent face of your investigation. You can protect her name, insist on an identified source or take only dates and custody stamps. Helping with her fare is a small kindness with its own cost, not a secret solution.

If you miss or refuse both events, the evening proceeds. The board can call the transfer uncontested; others can describe your absence as taking a side. You still gain access to the ledger and routing evidence later. Missing the witness costs a live account, rather than ending the game.

### The sender is real, and accountable

After the exchange, the document becomes available at the service table. Its older pages have eleven names cut out. The routing evidence identifies the invitation’s sender. There are **three alternative versions of the night**, chosen when a run is created. They are not three suspects who can all turn out to have sent the same invitation.

In **Carbon**, Mara sent it. She wanted an independent witness outside the old tenant group. Further conversation reveals a more personal need: she wanted someone with whom she had not already failed. Years earlier, she kept a carbon after an agreement to return records; Luca published gaps from which two people could be identified. Both violated the principle they demanded of the other. You can give Mara time to tell him, disclose it yourself or leave their history unresolved.

In **Proxy**, Celeste commissioned the witness. She wanted someone the board could not dismiss as staff, and also wanted the option of a neutral witness’s casting vote in a tied decision. A separate consent page exists, unsigned. You can refuse that role, accept it only to request an independent audit or leave it open. Her stated intent to prevent misuse is put against a document that has already misused your signature.

In **Dead Letter**, Inez released an old queued invitation intended for the previous tenant at your address. She was trying to trace the reused authorization. The former tenant is alive and has moved away; they have not mysteriously vanished. Inez can ask permission for contact, but does not give you their number on demand. Her mistake and the old tenant’s privacy do not cancel your right to an explanation. The precise signature history contains a contradiction discussed below.

You may confront the actual sender and set a limit, claim a debt or postpone the personal reckoning. Alternatively, keep the proof private and concentrate on the ledger. Knowing the sender explains the invitation; it does not make the method acceptable.

### Knowing people makes the decision harder

There is an optional second phase in which you can spend substantial time with up to two of the four leads, then return to them after deciding the ledger’s fate. You can also take the shorter route.

Mara’s work habits lead into a conflict over Sera’s emergency leave: protect a confidence, correct a timesheet, or disclose enough to seek a fairer arrangement. Celeste’s authority becomes tangible when an exhausted server asks to leave: refunds, another worker’s evening and her sister’s time compete. Luca’s warmth turns your interest into an agreement you never made; his acceptance of Celeste’s repair money complicates his claims of independence. Inez knows why Tom missed a shift but cannot correct a public accusation by freely disclosing his family’s private circumstances.

These are not extra clues revealing the sender. They give the central problem human scale. People can mean well and still leave somebody else paying for their explanation. Food, music, bad appliances, family conversations and small remembered preferences also get to matter for themselves.

Optional attraction and negotiated power sit within those lives. Celeste may give up control of an introduction under agreed terms. Luca may share a track and struggle to let it speak without explaining it. Mara may make room for company that has not earned its chair by fixing something. Inez offers a substantial non-romantic friendship. Refusing intimacy does not remove access to the mystery.

### The climax is custody, not a final accusation

There is no implemented confrontation that reveals one master villain behind everything. The climax asks what you will do with a dangerous, useful record.

You can **protect the names and release redacted accounts**. People regain some choice over corroboration; accountability slows and the board gains time. You can **publish the complete original through Luca**. Scrutiny becomes unavoidable, but tenant identities circulate beyond recall. You can **trade the original to Celeste for board access**. The access is real; so is the institution’s ability to present your cooperation as legitimacy. Or you can **keep the original**. Custody leaves you responsible without freezing anyone else’s plans.

Earlier promises affect how these decisions feel and how people respond. Publishing after promising Mara protection is a specific betrayal. Warmth with Celeste does not make a bargain independent. Sympathy for Luca does not automatically commit you to his preferred outcome. There is no romance score which chooses the document ending for you.

### The night ends; the larger story does not

The longer authored aftermath turns the theme back on you. Inez forwards a message she withheld “for your protection.” Whether you previously protected or exposed someone else changes the resonance. You can understand her intention while demanding the information, insist she ask next time, express anger or leave your answer until tomorrow.

Closing conversations can repair assumptions, establish distance, acknowledge disagreement or leave an invitation for another day. They do not require everyone to approve the ledger decision. At home, a remembered drink, refrigerator noise or radio preference can return beside the evidence. A private reflection lets the player say what stayed with them—or remain uncertain.

Dawn gives one of four written conclusions: **The Careful Copy**, **Open Circuit**, **Terms Accepted**, or **Private Property**. Each resolves the immediate custody decision and names a cost. None settles the whole future of the fund or the people in it.

There is also an optional Motel 27 hook: a room key, a cassette, two receipts and a photograph of your building before the flood. The photograph shows a doorway marked ROOM 06 where your window is now. Inez agrees to provide an inventory instead of demanding another unexplained appointment. **What the cassette contains, what the receipts prove and what the photograph ultimately means have not been developed into a second chapter.**

**Implemented — current text-route qualification.** The parser supports the evidence/custody endings, but its direct `sleep` action jumps to dawn and the matching conclusion. It does not automatically play the whole mirror, closing and reflection sequence above. Going home without settling custody produces a brief unresolved departure. The synopsis describes the registered authored story; the scene guide identifies the places where the text-led presentation does not reliably deliver that story yet.

### Who knows, who believes, who misleads

**Implemented — initial knowledge:** all four leads know the delivery-header distinction. The actual sender knows their authorship and matching proof. Inez knows about the signature and Motel forwarding. Mara knows the tenant risk and missing names. Celeste knows the tenant risk and board copy. Luca knows the tenant risk. The player begins without verified mystery facts; reading, hearing and showing evidence establish particular information.

**Implemented — initial beliefs:** in Carbon, Luca thinks Mara is selling the group out and Inez suspects Celeste sent the invitation. In Proxy, Luca thinks the board is recruiting a witness. In Dead Letter, Luca incorrectly believes he commissioned the invitation. These entries have an inference as their source; they are not rival versions of objective canon. The older bible also says Mara thinks Luca sent the Proxy invitation, but the initial runtime does **not** create that belief. Treat it as **Documented plan**, not an implemented memory.

**Implemented — explicit misleading conduct:** the player can falsely claim Luca’s invitation; Inez can report that claim; Luca can mistake sympathy for publication assent and later inflate interest into a tomorrow commitment. Mara admits withholding the historical carbon in her branch. Celeste discloses the intended proxy role only when asked further. Inez admits withholding a message. Distinguish an admitted concealment, a false inference and a deliberate player lie. The scripts do not prove that every incomplete answer is knowingly false.

**Implemented — sources with limits:** Ruth’s account and docket establish early movement. Ellis declines public use of their testimony; one separate request can secure a sealed account for an independent reviewer. The washroom allegation about relief money paying membership is explicitly unverified. Repeating it creates a rumour, not proof of fraud. Redaction and publication do not turn every allegation into fact.

### What remains unanswered

**Unresolved:** who first repurposed the signature; the complete authorization chain and missing clause; the exact parties and legal substance of the 00:20 transfer; who ordered the 00:19 movement; the identities and current wishes of all eleven removed people; the hospital-ticket guest’s accounting situation; the unknown saved-map name; the original sender of each anonymous supporting message; the driver’s “replacement taxi” warning; and the long-term outcomes of audit, publication or reform.

The Dead Letter texts need particular care. The opening identifies the signature as the protagonist’s delivery signature, and the runtime agrees. The queued letter is for the former tenant. Inez then says she expected to find the former tenant but found the person whose signature it was. That could be made coherent, but the actual document chronology is not supplied. **Do not silently decide that the protagonist is secretly the former tenant, or that either identity is supernatural.**

Motel 27 is described as a forwarding convention which can refer to a room or stored object; the parser also provides a concrete corridor and ordinary Room 06 lock. A place exists in the current map, but the sources do not settle whether every Motel reference names that same physical building.

## 3. THE CHARACTERS

### The protagonist

**Implemented:** an adult outsider with an apartment, a misused delivery signature and a chosen alias. The story gives them an ordinary home rather than a completed personal biography. Actions can establish promises, disclosures, moral habits and differing NPC impressions. A remembered fact is more useful to the story than a universal description of whether this is a “good” person.

**Documented plan:** a lightly defined protagonist, optional pronouns/presentation and background lenses such as service work, records work or practical work, including a complete unspecified option. No secret family connection, destined identity, mandatory trauma or pre-existing romance is approved by that brief. Broader multi-night identity development is not implemented merely because first-night saves persist.

### Mara Venn, 32 — the practical person who keeps the dangerous copy

**Implemented:** bar manager, tired repairer, attentive host, protector of tenant names; the sender in Carbon. The hidden scene says the old group removed eleven names to make people less findable, asking most of those people but not two who had already gone. Her Carbon confession complicates that protection with her own concealed copy. The player can promise discretion, betray it, respect her work, argue over Sera’s hours and repair—or limit—the habit of mistaking help for supervision.

**Documented plan:** former garment machinist and flood organiser; wants protection without deciding everybody’s life for them; fears that she has become the controlling custodian she distrusts. She feels others’ education gives them credibility she lacks. Her need is to be asked, not rescued. Mara and Luca’s old break concerns names and records, not an established failed romance.

**Voice and private life:** clipped practical speech that expands into logistical complaints; dry jokes, labels, pencils, soft chips, the exercise bike in her stairwell and the lamp she leaves on at home. She shows affection by remembering and saving ordinary things. She should remain capable of an impatient, unfair assumption, followed by a specific apology.

**Adult culture and relationships:** optional private attention and consent to company are implemented; competent kindness can become attraction, but serving her shift is not payment for intimacy. The current material does not establish a detailed personal fetish biography. Character-specific interests remain an authoring decision. Her ending can be repaired cooperation, bounded warmth or distance; the breakfast idea remains an invitation to decide later, not a booked date.

### Celeste Ardent, 41 — authority which wants to be believed on its own terms

**Implemented:** proprietor, witness recruiter, board participant and possible custodian of the original; the sender in Proxy. She knows about the board duplicate. Her branch exposes a prepared casting-vote option. She can correct wording, allow an objection and still benefit from the institutional arrangement being questioned. Later scenes show real choices about Noor’s relief, family time, refunds and the consequences of her repair gift to Luca.

**Documented plan:** a former commercial mediator who acquired Velvet from her estranged mother; resents inherited obligations while using inherited authority. She fears replaceability and wants respect without dependence. She underestimates how costly exit can be for someone with less money or status. Mara’s fair pay does not purchase loyalty; Inez’s authority cannot simply be bought. “The board keeps copies of all patron agreements” is a wider dossier claim; the ledger duplicate is the concrete implemented disclosure.

**Voice and private life:** exact nouns, controlled sentences, literal corrections; taped reading glasses, three uselessly distant spare pairs, a disappointing sandwich, an overly assertive pickle and renovation television discussed with her sister. Her humour works when precision meets something she cannot organise.

**Adult culture and relationships:** the clearest current connection to negotiated authority and role reversal. She can surrender control of an introduction within explicit limits, or join a small shared ritual of setting work aside. Those scenes do not establish that she has a universal dominant/submissive identity. Personal attraction can be returned or gently bounded; professional warmth is a full result. No public romantic performance or disclosure-as-payment is part of her approved character direction.

### Luca Serrin, 29 — a generous storyteller who improves the wrong sentences

**Implemented:** radio archivist seeking public evidence; never the actual invitation sender in the three current variants. His initial belief can be wrong. He wants names protected in principle, but dates can identify people even after names disappear. In Carbon, Mara says his published gaps exposed two identities. The repair gift from Celeste has no editorial-control term; that does not make concerns about independence vanish. His later arc centres on inventing a commitment from the player’s interest, correcting it and apologising without buying forgiveness.

**Documented plan:** tenant electrician turned investigator; needs his story to justify what he asked of friends. He fears being an opportunist with a good slogan and assumes public disclosure helps until his own source is endangered. His confidential source promise and Celeste’s earlier anonymous server support are dossier background; the playable repair-funding dispute supplies a specific enacted version of the tension.

**Voice and private life:** quick jokes, fragments, self-corrections and explanations which outstay their welcome. Dead phone, missing adapters, a microwave grievance which is genuinely in the music credits, a playlist and a promised call to his father. He becomes more sincere when he leaves the final joke unsaid.

**Adult culture and relationships:** shared listening, private attention and choosing whether to watch or participate are implemented. His interest in an audience is not permission to expose someone else. A possible date requires reciprocal attention, repaired conduct and sufficient trust; friendship or distance are valid. More specific fetish interests are **Unresolved**, not inferable from being a performer.

### Inez Vale, 56 — care, fallible memory and the end of a shift

**Implemented:** custodian, keeper of practical records, source of the printer explanation and Motel lead; sender in Dead Letter. She knows the signature was reused. Her certainty is not infallible: the later trolley-colour mistake is visibly corrected. She can withhold information for protective reasons and apologise. The new local envelope arrangement adds an actual acknowledged promise with custody, a deadline, missed time and repair.

**Documented plan:** former night-office clerk and hotel receptionist; retained a register without telling her employer. Wants it kept from becoming an access list. Fears mistaking silence for integrity and needs others to share responsibility. Respects Mara, finds Luca careless and can confront Celeste over employment obligations.

**Voice and private life:** short, literal sentences and practical silences. Mismatched gloves, borrowed cups, failed-recipe radio, a sister who wants her home and an extra shift she expects to be paid for. Her funniest lines puncture mystification: the old system is still used because the replacement charges per message.

**Adult culture and relationships:** **non-romantic**. Her intimate material is friendship, discretion, concern, a right to decline supervision and an introduction that does not reduce the protagonist to “the witness.” Care is part of the venue’s culture and of her ordinary working life; it does not make her everyone’s endlessly available caretaker. No romantic or jealousy route is established for her.

**Implemented — authored supporting roles:** Ruth, 48, witnesses the trolley movement and needs her bus; Ellis, 37, refuses public testimony; the former tenant in Dead Letter is alive and has their own contact limits. ### Where the written story stops

**Implemented:** four terminal first-night scene texts, plus a developed but optional Motel teaser and the brief unresolved parser departure. The climax and immediate endings are established; it would be incorrect to say the project has no ending.

**Documented plan:** later nights, further districts (Meat Market, Saint, Glasshouse and the Static), and DEJA//VU as an extension point. The recent direction prioritises deeper first-night play before producing another chapter.

**Unresolved:** there is no authored final ending for the whole future city story, no developed Motel investigation, no resolved cassette/receipt/photo payload, and no playable follow-through for the seven-day audit or board access. There are no additional fully specified but unimplemented ending branches in the reviewed plans. The future scope is a direction, not a hidden completed plot.

## 6. CREATIVE DECISIONS

### The three decisions with the largest effect

1. **Define the mystery’s promised resolution. — Unresolved.** The sender is solved, but the precise institutional act, signature misuse and document chronology are not all solved. Decide which questions Chapter One must answer, which are deliberately retained, and what concrete harm the transfer threatens. Resolve the Dead Letter identity/signature sequence before adding more clues. This will make the existing climax feel earned rather than like a choice made beside an unfinished explanation.
2. **Choose the emotional route the opening must actually deliver. — Unresolved.** The manuscript has a rich arc; the parser can skip its human aftermath, reject replies to its own questions and repeat introductions. Decide the minimum satisfying sequence from first greeting to personal consequence, including which scenes remain optional. The [transition milestone](TEXT-LED-TRANSITION.md) is a bounded implementation proposal for later, not work undertaken here.
3. **Give the adult identity and private voice specific dramatic content. — Documented plan needing decisions.** The present story is strongest on institutional permission and ordinary care. Decide which compatible character-specific adult desires, pleasures, limits and awkward moments make Velvet compelling as a kink-centred place to return to. Keep room for sincerity, mundane friendship and unproductive fun. A theme registry cannot make these decisions for you, and every scene should not teach the same lesson about consent.
